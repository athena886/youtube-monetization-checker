How Much Do YouTubers Make? By Channel Size (2026)

Subscriber count does not generate a paycheck. Views, viewer location, niche, format, season, and monetization mix do. These tables turn those variables into transparent monthly scenarios instead of promising a fixed rate.

Quick answer

In the illustrative ranges below, a newly monetized small channel may earn roughly $15–$500 per month from 10,000–100,000 monthly views, while a 100K–1M-subscriber channel might model about $500–$30,000 from 250,000–3 million views. A 1M+ channel can fall below or far above that range. These are estimated platform-revenue scenarios, not typical salaries or guarantees.

How Much Do YouTubers Make Per 1,000 Views?

There is no universal YouTube pay rate per 1,000 views. The most useful creator-side metric is RPM, which YouTube defines as total revenue reported in Analytics per 1,000 views after revenue share. For Shorts, the denominator is 1,000 engaged views. RPM can include ads, YouTube Premium, memberships, Super Chat, and Super Stickers, so it is broader than ad revenue alone.

CPM is different. CPM represents advertiser cost per 1,000 ad impressions before YouTube’s revenue share and counts only monetized advertising impressions. RPM includes all eligible views, including views on which no ad appeared, which is one reason RPM is usually lower than CPM.

Estimated earnings = monthly views ÷ 1,000 × RPM

Example: 200,000 monthly views ÷ 1,000 × $4 RPM = $800 estimated monthly platform revenue.

Small vs Big Channels: What Actually Changes

A larger subscriber base can improve the odds of repeat views, but advertisers do not pay simply because a channel crossed 10K, 100K, or 1M subscribers. A channel’s current monthly audience and monetized playback mix are more important than its historical subscriber total.

Audience geography is one of the largest variables. Advertiser competition tends to be stronger in markets with higher commercial demand. Topic also matters: a viewer researching accounting software, investments, or a business purchase can be more valuable to an advertiser than a casual entertainment view. Seasonality, video length, ad suitability, viewer age, device, and the percentage of views that are monetized can all move the final RPM.

For public long-form videos covered by the Watch Page Monetization Module, YouTube’s official terms state that partners receive 55% of net advertising revenues. That is not the same as “55% of CPM” for every view, because CPM measures advertiser-facing impressions and many views have no monetized impression. Shorts use a separate Creator Pool allocation and a 45% creator share.

Big channels also have more opportunities outside platform RPM. Sponsorships, affiliate links, licensing, courses, products, live events, and services can exceed AdSense revenue. Those streams are not included in this page’s tables because their deal terms are private and vary far more than YouTube platform revenue.

How These Numbers Are Estimated

The channel-size table uses a transparent scenario model rather than claiming access to creators’ private income. First, it assigns a broad monthly-view envelope to each subscriber label. Second, it applies an RPM envelope aligned with the niche presets in YPPCheck’s calculator. The low estimate is low views multiplied by low RPM; the high estimate is high views multiplied by high RPM.

The niche ordering is based on public benchmark patterns rather than an official YouTube category rate card. Finance and technology generally attract advertisers with higher customer values; gaming, broad entertainment, and ambient content often have lower blended advertiser demand. Kids content has additional advertising and personalization constraints. Shorts are separated because their pooled revenue model is not comparable to Watch Page ads.

For definitions and platform rules, this guide relies on YouTube Help’s RPM and CPM explanation and partner earnings overview. Industry ranges are estimates, not audited earnings records. Individual outcomes can fall outside every range shown here.

FAQ

How much does a YouTuber with 10K subscribers make?

Subscriber count alone cannot answer that question. In this page’s scenario, a channel around the 10K boundary with 50,000–100,000 monthly views and a $1.50–$5 RPM might model roughly $75–$500 per month in platform revenue. Actual results can be lower or higher.

How much does a YouTuber with 100K subscribers make?

A 100K-subscriber channel could be inactive or receive millions of monthly views. Using 250,000–500,000 monthly views and a $2–$10 RPM produces a wide $500–$5,000 planning range. Sponsorships and other off-platform income are separate.

How much does a YouTuber with 1 million subscribers make?

There is no fixed million-subscriber salary. At 1–10 million monthly views and a $2–$12 RPM, the modeled platform range is approximately $2,000–$120,000 or more. The spread is intentionally wide because niche, geography, activity, and view volume dominate the result.

Do YouTubers get paid per view?

Not at a fixed per-view rate. Revenue comes from eligible monetization features, and RPM summarizes reported creator revenue per 1,000 total views. Some views have ads, some do not, and advertiser bids differ.

How do I check if a channel is monetized?

Only the owner can confirm YPP status in YouTube Studio. A public tool can compare visible statistics with YPP thresholds but cannot see private watch hours, revenue, policy standing, or enrollment. Use the monetization checker as a public-signal estimate.

Estimate, not income advice

Every earnings number on this page is illustrative. It is not official YouTube data, a guarantee, or financial advice. Actual revenue varies by channel and period, may include or exclude different products in Analytics, and can be reduced by taxes, fees, invalid-traffic adjustments, claims, and operating costs.
